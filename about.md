I'm a master's student in Speech and Language Processing at the University of
Edinburgh, in the Institute for Language, Cognition and Computation.

My work keeps coming back to one problem: knowing when a conversational system
should speak. Dialogue systems usually wait out a fixed stretch of silence and
treat it as permission. People don't. We read prosody, syntax and rhythm to tell
when a speaker is about to finish, and we have a reply ready before they do. My
dissertation, supervised by Catherine Lai and Sarenne Wallbridge, is on
predicting that moment — modelling when a speaker is about to give up the floor,
rather than waiting for silence to confirm it after the fact.

I came to this from biology. My first degree was in Natural Sciences at
Birmingham, so I met machine learning through living systems before I met it
through language. That order still shapes what I care about. I'm most interested
where models of language and models of biology overlap: foundation models for
the life sciences, and the noisy, high-dimensional signals both fields are stuck
working with. Some of the work here sits on that boundary.

Computing is the family trade. My grandfather programmed early printers; my
father writes iOS software. I treat my own move into machine learning as the
next entry in that line.

Outside research I play guitar, spend more time than I can defend on ear
training and music theory, and have a weakness for games that take their writing
seriously.
